Water as a resource

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Water is a renewable natural resource that supports every living system and every community. People drink it, prepare food with it, wash with it, grow crops, manufacture products, generate electricity and maintain ecosystems. Water moves continuously, but usable water is limited by location, quality, timing and cost.

Why water matters

A resource is something people or ecosystems value and use. Water has environmental value because wetlands, rivers and groundwater support habitats. It has social and cultural value because communities depend on water for health, recreation, identity and connection to Country. It also has economic value because farming, mining, manufacturing, tourism and energy production require reliable supplies.

Renewable does not mean unlimited

The water cycle renews water by moving it between the ocean, atmosphere, land and groundwater. However, a river or aquifer can still be depleted when people take water faster than rainfall and groundwater recharge replace it. Pollution can also make water unsafe even when the total volume has not changed.

Competing needs

Managers must balance water for households, farms, industry, First Nations cultural values and river ecosystems. During drought, one use can reduce the water available for another, so decisions involve both efficiency and fairness.

Key terms

  • resource
  • renewable resource
  • water security
  • potable water

Earth’s water distribution

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Earth looks like a water-rich planet because water covers about 71% of its surface. Yet most of that water is saline. The small freshwater share is mainly frozen or underground, so only a tiny fraction is immediately available in rivers, lakes and wetlands.

Saltwater and freshwater

Oceans, seas and saline bays hold about 96.5% of all water. Freshwater makes up only a few per cent. Of that freshwater, about 68.7% is stored in ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow, while about 30.1% is fresh groundwater.

Accessible surface water

Fresh lakes contain about 0.26% of the world’s freshwater and rivers contain about 0.006%. These small stores are extremely important because people can reach and use them more easily than deep groundwater or polar ice.

Why percentages matter

A percentage of total water and a percentage of freshwater are different measures. For example, fresh groundwater is about 30.1% of freshwater but only about 0.76% of all water on Earth.

Freshwater sources and groundwater

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Freshwater occurs as surface water, frozen water, groundwater and rainwater. Each source has different advantages, limits and rates of renewal. Understanding where water is stored helps explain why some places have reliable supply while others depend on dams, wells or seasonal rain.

Surface and frozen water

Surface water includes rivers, streams, lakes, reservoirs and wetlands. It is visible and often easy to collect, but it can evaporate or become polluted. Frozen water in glaciers and snowfields stores large volumes, but it is remote and releases water seasonally through melting.

Groundwater

Some precipitation infiltrates soil and rock. It fills spaces and fractures in an aquifer. The water table marks the upper surface of the saturated zone. Above it, the unsaturated zone contains both air and water. Wells provide access, but pumping faster than recharge lowers the water table.

Rainwater

Rain can be collected from roofs and stored in tanks. Rainwater reduces demand on treated mains water, but supply varies with rainfall, tank size and household use. Safe drinking may require treatment and careful maintenance.

Human activities that contribute

Over-pumping
Lowers the water table when extraction exceeds recharge.
Leaking waste or chemicals
Contaminants can infiltrate soil and enter an aquifer.
Urban paving
Impermeable surfaces reduce infiltration and groundwater recharge.

Key terms

  • aquifer
  • water table
  • saturated zone
  • unsaturated zone
  • infiltration
  • recharge

Water footprints and hidden water

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Direct water use is the water people see flowing from a tap. A water footprint also includes the water used to grow, process and transport the products people consume. This hidden or virtual water can be much larger than household tap use.

Three parts of a water footprint

Green water is rainwater stored in soil and used by plants. Blue water comes from rivers, lakes or groundwater and is consumed or moved. Grey water is the amount needed to dilute pollution to an agreed standard. These categories show that a product’s impact depends on both quantity and location.

Products and supply chains

Cotton needs water while growing, and more water is used during cleaning, dyeing and manufacturing. Beef has a large footprint because water supports pasture, feed crops and animal care. A product made in a water-stressed area can create greater pressure than the same product made where supply is abundant.

Using footprint data carefully

Footprint figures are global averages, not exact labels for every item. Climate, farming methods, crop yield, factory efficiency and pollution controls can change the result.

How people use water

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

People use water for household, agricultural, industrial, recreational and environmental purposes. The proportion used by each sector varies because countries have different climates, economies, technologies and populations.

Agriculture

Irrigation supplies crops when rainfall is insufficient or unreliable. Livestock also need drinking water, and farms use water for cleaning and processing. Agriculture usually dominates withdrawals in regions with dry climates and large irrigated areas.

Industry and households

Industry uses water for cooling, washing, mining, manufacturing and energy production. Municipal use includes homes and services. Households use water for drinking, cooking, toilets, showers, washing, cleaning and gardens.

Recreation and the environment

Rivers, lakes and reservoirs support swimming, boating and tourism. Environmental water remains in or is released to rivers to maintain wetlands, fish breeding, water quality and floodplain vegetation.

Conserving water at home

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Household conservation reduces demand on dams, rivers and treatment plants. It can also save the energy used to pump, heat and treat water. The greatest savings come from efficient fittings, shorter use and fixing leaks rather than from occasional small actions alone.

Bathrooms and laundries

Showers, toilets and washing machines are major indoor uses. Efficient showerheads, shorter showers, dual-flush toilets and full washing loads reduce demand. A WELS rating helps compare appliance efficiency.

Kitchens and gardens

Dishwashers should run with full loads. Keeping cold water in the fridge avoids running the tap while it cools. Mulch, local plants and watering during cooler parts of the day reduce evaporation in gardens.

Measuring improvement

A household audit records shower length, appliance ratings, leaks and outdoor watering. Comparing use over time shows whether changes work. A target should account for household size and property size.

  • water conservation
  • water efficiency
  • WELS rating
  • dual flush

The water cycle

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between the ocean, atmosphere, land, living things and groundwater. Solar energy drives evaporation and gravity moves precipitation, runoff and groundwater downhill.

Atmospheric processes

Evaporation changes liquid water into vapour. Plants add vapour through transpiration. As moist air rises and cools, condensation forms tiny droplets or ice crystals in clouds. When particles grow heavy enough, precipitation falls as rain, snow, sleet or hail.

Movement on and below land

Runoff flows across the surface into streams and rivers. Infiltration moves water into soil. Percolation carries it deeper to aquifers, while groundwater flow can slowly return it to rivers or the ocean. Plants take water through their roots and release some by transpiration.

Stores and flows

A store holds water, such as an ocean, lake, glacier or aquifer. A flow transfers water between stores. Water may stay in an ocean for a long time but move quickly through the atmosphere after evaporation.

Human activities that contribute

Urban paving
Reduces infiltration and increases rapid runoff.
Vegetation clearing
Reduces interception and transpiration while exposing soil.
Groundwater pumping
Moves water out of underground stores faster than natural discharge.

Key terms

  • evaporation
  • transpiration
  • condensation
  • precipitation
  • runoff
  • infiltration

Catchments and drainage basins

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

A catchment, also called a drainage basin, is the area of land where water drains to a common outlet. Rainfall flows downhill through small channels and tributaries into a main river, lake, reservoir or estuary. A watershed is the high land that separates neighbouring catchments.

Catchment boundaries

Ridges and higher ground form drainage divides. Water falling on opposite sides of a watershed enters different river systems. Catchments are nested: a small creek catchment can sit inside a much larger river basin.

What affects runoff

Steep slopes speed up runoff. Permeable soil and vegetation increase infiltration and slow the flow. Intense rain, saturated soil, cleared land and paved surfaces create more rapid runoff and a greater flood risk.

Catchment health

Activities anywhere upstream can affect downstream water. Fertiliser, sediment, rubbish, sewage and industrial chemicals can enter runoff. Protecting vegetation, controlling erosion and treating wastewater improve water quality.

Human activities that contribute

Land clearing
Exposes soil, increases erosion and speeds runoff.
Construction and roads
Create impermeable surfaces and carry sediment into streams.
Farming and industry
Can add nutrients, pesticides, waste and chemicals to runoff.

Key terms

  • catchment
  • drainage basin
  • watershed
  • tributary
  • runoff

Sydney’s water supply and Warragamba Dam

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Greater Sydney’s water supply begins with rainfall in protected catchments. Creeks and rivers carry raw water into dams. WaterNSW stores and transfers it, filtration plants treat it, and Sydney Water distributes safe drinking water through pipes.

The connected system

Weather, climate and rainfall

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions such as today’s rain, temperature and wind. Climate describes the long-term pattern for a place, usually measured over decades. Climate influences how much freshwater is available and when it arrives.

Why rainfall varies

Latitude affects solar heating and atmospheric circulation. Coastal location supplies moisture, mountains force air to rise and cool, and ocean–atmosphere patterns such as El Niño and La Niña change rainfall from year to year.

Relief rainfall and rain shadows

Moist air rising over a mountain cools and condenses, so the windward slope receives more rain. Descending air warms and dries on the leeward side, producing a rain shadow.

Climate variability

An average does not mean every year is similar. Australia can move between drought and flood. Water managers therefore use long records, forecasts and storage data rather than relying on one wet or dry month.

Global rainfall distribution

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Rainfall is uneven across the world. Many equatorial areas are wet, subtropical desert belts are dry, monsoon regions have strongly seasonal rain, and mountain ranges create wet windward slopes and dry rain shadows.

Global controls

Strong heating near the equator causes rising moist air and frequent convectional rain. Descending air near 30° north and south helps create dry desert regions. Prevailing winds and distance from oceans influence how much moisture reaches land.

Regional examples

The Amazon and parts of Southeast Asia receive high rainfall. North Africa and much of West Asia are dry. Northern Australia has a summer wet season, while southern Australia receives more rain from cool-season frontal systems.

Maps and generalisations

A small-scale world map shows broad patterns but can hide local variations caused by mountains, coastlines and cities. Always use the legend and describe locations precisely.

Water stress and water scarcity

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Water stress occurs when demand places strong pressure on available renewable supply. Water scarcity means there is insufficient water of suitable quality to meet needs. Stress is often measured as withdrawals compared with renewable supply.

Understanding the measure

A country using a high share of its renewable supply has little spare capacity. WRI classifies high stress as withdrawing 40% or more and extremely high stress as withdrawing at least 80%. A short drought can then cause severe shortages.

Scarcity is more than rainfall

Demand, pollution, damaged infrastructure and unfair distribution can create scarcity. Water quality matters because contaminated water cannot safely meet household or ecosystem needs.

Timescale

Annual averages can hide seasonal stress. A region may have enough water over a year but face a severe dry-season shortage because rainfall and demand occur at different times.

Human activity can increase demand, reduce supply or damage water quality. Scarcity becomes more likely when population and production grow without efficient use, pollution control or limits on extraction.

Effects of water scarcity

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Water scarcity affects health, education, livelihoods, food production, ecosystems and social stability. The impacts are unequal because low-income and rural communities often have fewer alternatives.

People and wellbeing

Unsafe water increases diarrhoeal and other water-related disease. Time spent collecting water can reduce school attendance and paid work, with women and girls often carrying most of the burden. Short supply also limits hygiene.

Economy and food

Farmers may plant smaller areas, lose livestock or pay more for irrigation. Industries can reduce production when water is unavailable. Higher costs can raise food and energy prices.

Ecosystems

Low river flow shrinks wetlands, warms water and concentrates pollution. Fish breeding, waterbird habitat and floodplain vegetation can decline. Pumping groundwater can also reduce springs and river baseflow.

Australia’s water availability and use

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Australia has highly variable rainfall and large dry interiors. Water is unevenly distributed in space and time, so storages, groundwater and transfers help supply cities and agriculture. National totals can change sharply between wet and dry years.

Climate and geography

Coastal and elevated regions generally receive more reliable rain than the interior. High temperatures increase evaporation, and many inland rivers have variable flow. Australia therefore experiences both long droughts and major floods.

Who uses water

Agriculture is the largest consuming industry because irrigation supports crops and pasture. Households use a much smaller national volume, but reliable urban water requires large catchments, dams, treatment and pipe networks.

Current conditions

Water managers track rainfall, soil moisture, streamflow, storages and groundwater. In November 2026, some eastern and southern storages were at or below half capacity and streamflow was below average at many southern sites.

Murrurundi drought case study

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Murrurundi in the Upper Hunter experienced severe water insecurity during the 2017–20 drought. Local supplies failed, the town reached Day Zero and water had to be trucked in. The case shows how drought, small storages and limited connections can expose a town to physical scarcity.

The crisis

After years of low rainfall, local sources could no longer meet even restricted demand. Level 6 restrictions limited residents to about 140 litres per person per day, and carted water kept the town operating.

Longer-term response

A pipeline connected Murrurundi to the Scone water system. Authorities also completed a 1,650 kL reservoir in 2021, increasing local storage and improving security for future droughts.

Lessons

Emergency carting protects health but is costly and temporary. A stronger response combines connected supplies, storage, conservation, leak control, drought planning and communication.

Greywater, rainwater and recycling

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Alternative water sources reduce demand for treated drinking water. Greywater comes from showers, baths, hand basins and washing machines, but excludes toilet waste. Rainwater is collected from roofs, while recycled water is treated wastewater supplied for suitable uses.

Matching quality to use

Drinking water requires very high treatment standards. Gardens, toilet flushing and some industrial uses may use lower-quality water if health and environmental rules are followed. Using the right quality for the right task saves high-quality supply.

Greywater safety

Greywater should be used quickly, kept away from edible plant parts and not allowed to form pools. Harsh detergents can damage soil. Local rules determine whether a simple diversion or a treatment system is required.

Benefits and limits

Alternative sources can reduce mains demand and stormwater runoff. However, tanks and treatment systems cost money, require maintenance and may produce little water during drought.

River systems and landforms

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

A river system transfers water, sediment and nutrients from higher land to a lake, sea or inland basin. The source lies near the beginning of the river, tributaries join the main channel, and the mouth is where the river ends.

Upper, middle and lower course

Upper courses are often steep, narrow and fast flowing, with erosion cutting valleys. Middle courses become wider and may develop meanders. Lower courses have gentler gradients, broad floodplains and greater deposition.

Landforms

A meander is a bend created as faster water erodes the outside bank and slower water deposits sediment on the inside. A floodplain is flat land built by river sediment during floods. A delta forms where deposition at the mouth creates distributary channels.

A connected system

Changes upstream affect downstream flow and sediment. Dams trap sediment, clearing increases erosion, and channel modification can speed water or disconnect floodplains.

Floodplains, floods and river risk

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Flooding occurs when river discharge exceeds channel capacity and water spreads across the floodplain. Floods are natural parts of river systems, but damage increases when people place homes and infrastructure in exposed areas.

Why floods happen

Intense or prolonged rain, saturated soil, snowmelt and cyclones can increase runoff. Steep slopes and impermeable surfaces move water quickly into channels. A large catchment may also receive rain in several tributaries at once.

Benefits

Floods deposit fertile sediment, refill wetlands and connect rivers with floodplain habitats. Many plants, fish and waterbirds depend on periodic flooding.

Managing risk

Levees, dams and drainage can reduce some floods but cannot remove all risk. Warning systems, evacuation plans, flood-compatible buildings, land-use zoning and restored wetlands reduce exposure and vulnerability.

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

The Murray–Darling Basin covers about one million square kilometres across Queensland, New South Wales, the Australian Capital Territory, Victoria and South Australia. Its rivers support communities, farms, wetlands, wildlife and more than 50 First Nations.

A large connected basin

The Basin contains internationally important wetlands and habitat for native fish and waterbirds. Water Country connects First Nations peoples with law, identity, food, stories, ceremony and responsibility to care for Country.

Murray–Darling pressures and management

ReadingReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

The Basin faces variable rainfall, drought, floods, irrigation demand, river regulation, salinity, invasive species and climate change. Management aims to share limited water while maintaining healthy rivers and communities.

Key pressures

Large withdrawals can reduce river flow and floodplain inundation. Dams change seasonal patterns and trap sediment. Land clearing and irrigation can raise salinity, while fertiliser and erosion reduce water quality. Warmer conditions increase evaporation and ecological stress.

The Basin Plan

The Basin Plan sets sustainable diversion limits for how much water can be taken. Governments recover water for the environment and coordinate planning across Basin states. Environmental water holders release water to support wetlands, fish, vegetation and water quality.

Evaluating management

Effectiveness should be judged using ecological, cultural, social and economic evidence. One wet year does not prove long-term success. Monitoring must separate management effects from natural rainfall variability.
